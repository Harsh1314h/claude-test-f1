01

What is Formula 1?

Formula 1 is the top level of single-seater circuit racing, run by the FIA (the sport's governing body). The "formula" is the rulebook every car must obey: size, weight, engine, aerodynamics, safety.

Each season has around 24 Grands Prix around the world. There are two championships running at once:

Drivers' Championship

Every driver scores points for themselves. Most points at the end of the season = World Champion.

Constructors' Championship

Each team (constructor) builds its own chassis and runs two cars. Both drivers' points are added together. Prize money depends on this table.

Deep dive: tie-breaks, fastest lap and short races
  • If drivers finish level on points, the one with more wins is ahead; then more 2nd places, and so on.
  • From 2019 to 2024 a bonus point went to the driver with the fastest lap (if they finished in the top 10). That bonus was dropped from 2025.
  • If a race is stopped early and can't restart, reduced points are given depending on how much distance was completed.
  • In 2026 an eleventh team joined, making the grid 22 cars.

The race weekend

A Grand Prix runs over three days. Most are a standard weekend. A handful each year are Sprint weekends with an extra short race on Saturday.

Qualifying: the knockout

Qualifying sets the starting order (the grid). It is split into three knockout sessions, and the slowest cars drop out after each one. Press play to watch it happen.

    The race and grid penalties

    A Grand Prix is the fewest laps that add up to more than 305 km (Monaco is the exception at about 260 km), with a two-hour limit on racing time. Drivers can start lower than they qualified because of grid penalties: for example, using more power-unit or gearbox parts than the season allows, or causing a collision in a previous race.

    Deep dive: parc fermé, the 107% rule and penalty points
    • Parc fermé: from qualifying onwards, teams can make only very limited changes to the car's set-up. Break parc fermé and you start from the pit lane.
    • 107% rule: a driver whose best Q1 time is more than 107% of the fastest Q1 time may be refused a start, although stewards can allow it (e.g. after a crash).
    • Penalty points: offences add points to a driver's superlicence. 12 points within 12 months = a one-race ban.
    • In-race penalties: 5 or 10 second time penalties (served at a pit stop or added to the result), drive-through, stop-go, or disqualification.

    04

    Aerodynamics: downforce vs drag

    An F1 car's wings work like an aeroplane wing upside-down. Instead of lift they make downforce, pushing the tyres into the track so the car can corner much faster than grip alone would allow.

    The cost is drag: air resistance that slows the car on the straights. Every setup is a trade-off between the two.

    Downforce
    Drag

    Bars show relative values (illustrative). Both forces grow with the square of speed: double the speed, four times the force.

    Front wing

    First part to meet the air. Makes downforce and, just as important, steers the airflow around the front tyres and towards the floor.

    Rear wing

    Big, adjustable-angle wing. Steeper = more grip in corners, more drag on straights.

    Floor & ground effect

    Since 2022 most downforce comes from underneath. Tunnels under the floor narrow like a Venturi, so air speeds up and its pressure drops, sucking the car to the road.

    Diffuser

    The upswept exit at the back. It slows the fast underfloor air gradually back to normal speed, which keeps the pressure under the car low.

    Deep dive: porpoising and ride height

    Ground-effect downforce rises sharply as the car gets closer to the track. If the floor gets too close, the airflow can stall, downforce suddenly drops, the car rises, downforce returns and the car is pulled down again. This bouncing cycle is called porpoising and was a big problem in 2022. Teams run the car stiff and as low as they dare; a wooden-composite plank under the car is measured after the race to stop them running too low.

    In theory, at high speed an F1 car makes more downforce than its own weight, which is why people say it could drive upside-down on a ceiling.

    05

    DRS: the overtaking aid

    DRS (Drag Reduction System) was used from 2011 to 2025. On certain straights, a chasing driver could open a flap in the rear wing. This "stalls" the wing, which cuts drag and adds top speed so they can attack the car ahead.

    Detection pointAre you within 1 second of the car ahead?
    Activation zonePress the button on a marked straight: the flap opens.
    BrakingBrake or lift off and the flap snaps shut.
    Speed305km/h
    • The slot gap could open up to 85 mm.
    • Not allowed in the first two laps of a race, just after a Safety Car restart, or when race control disables it (e.g. rain).
    • Qualifying and practice: free to use in the zones on any lap.
    2026 update: active aerodynamics

    From 2026, DRS is gone. Both the front and rear wings have movable elements that every driver can switch into a low-drag "straight" setting on designated straights, then back to high-downforce for corners. For overtaking there is now an extra electrical-energy boost for a driver within one second of the car ahead. The animation shows the classic 2011–2025 DRS flap.

    06

    The hybrid power unit

    Since 2014, F1 cars have used a 1.6-litre V6 turbo hybrid. It is not just an engine; it is six connected parts called the power unit (PU). The car is now in x-ray view: pick a mode and watch the energy move.

    Fuel / combustion Exhaust heat Electrical energy Drive to wheels
    Power split

    ICE

    Internal Combustion Engine: 1.6 L V6, single turbo, rev-limited to 15,000 rpm.

    TC

    Turbocharger. Exhaust spins a turbine, which drives a compressor that forces extra air into the engine.

    MGU-K

    Motor Generator Unit – Kinetic. Harvests energy under braking; gives it back as up to 120 kW (≈160 hp) of boost (2014–25).

    MGU-H

    Motor Generator Unit – Heat. Sits on the turbo shaft and turns exhaust heat into electricity. It also spins the turbo up to kill turbo lag. Removed for 2026.

    ES

    Energy Store: the battery, under the fuel cell.

    CE

    Control Electronics: manages how energy moves around the system.

    Deep dive: efficiency and the 2026 reset

    F1 power units have passed 50% thermal efficiency, meaning more than half the fuel's energy becomes useful work. For comparison, a road-car petrol engine is typically around 30–40%. Fuel flow is limited (100 kg/hour in 2014–25), so power comes from efficiency, not just burning more fuel.

    For 2026, the MGU-H was dropped, the MGU-K grew to 350 kW, and power is roughly half combustion, half electric. Cars run on fully sustainable fuel.

    07

    Tyres: grip is temporary

    Tyres are the only part of the car that touches the track. One supplier (Pirelli) makes them, and at each race three dry compounds are chosen from its range and named by colour. Tap a compound to fit it to the car.

    Degradation: why lap times rise

    As a tyre wears and overheats, it loses grip and lap times go up. Softer tyres start faster but fade sooner. Near the end of their life some fall off a "cliff".

    Illustrative model. Real degradation depends on track, temperature, fuel load and driving style.

    • In a dry race each driver must use at least two different dry compounds, so at least one pit stop.
    • If the race is declared wet (inters or wets used), that rule no longer applies.
    • Tyres only work in a narrow temperature window, which is why drivers weave to warm them behind the Safety Car.
    Deep dive: compounds and wet-weather tyres

    Pirelli's dry range runs from C1 (hardest) to C6 (softest, added in 2025). At each event three are nominated and labelled Hard (white), Medium (yellow) and Soft (red), so a "soft" at one track may be a harder compound than the "soft" at another. The grooved wet tyres clear large amounts of water: Pirelli says the full wet can move about 85 litres per second at 300 km/h, the intermediate around 30.

    Pit stops & strategy

    A modern stop to change four tyres takes around 2 to 3 seconds of standing still, done by a crew of about 20. The real cost is the whole trip through the pit lane at the speed limit (usually 80 km/h): typically about 20 seconds lost compared with staying out.

    Pit entry0.0s

    Undercut vs overcut

    Two cars, one second apart. Who gets ahead after both have stopped?

    Undercut

    Pit first. Fresh tyres are much faster for a few laps, so by the time your rival pits you've gained enough to come out ahead.

    Overcut

    Stay out longer. Works when new tyres are slow to warm up, when the rival rejoins in traffic, or when your old tyres are still strong.

    The Safety Car effect

    Normal stop
    ~20 s
    Under Safety Car

    Behind the Safety Car the whole field is slow, so a stop costs roughly half as much time. That's why you see mass pit stops when it comes out. Exact figures vary by track.

    09

    Flags & safety

    Marshals around the track (and LED panels) use flags to talk to drivers. Tap a flag to see what it means.

    When things go wrong

    SC Safety Car

    A real car leads the field at reduced speed while an incident is cleared. No overtaking; the pack bunches up, wiping out gaps.

    VSC Virtual Safety Car

    No physical car. Every driver must stay above a minimum lap-time "delta", slowing by a similar amount everywhere, so gaps are roughly kept.

    RED Red flag

    Session stopped. Cars return slowly to the pit lane. Teams may change tyres and do some repairs before the restart.

    The halo

    The titanium hoop above the cockpit, highlighted on the right, has been compulsory since 2018. It weighs only around 7 kg but is tested to withstand loads comparable to the weight of a double-decker bus. It deflects debris, wheels and other cars away from the driver's head, and has been credited with saving lives in several major crashes.

    Deep dive: other safety systems
    • HANS device: a carbon collar linking the helmet to the driver's shoulders, preventing extreme head movement in impacts.
    • Wheel tethers: high-strength cables keep wheels attached to the car in a crash.
    • Medical car & extraction: a medical car follows the first lap and responds to crashes; seats can be lifted out with the driver strapped in.
    • Fire-resistant suits: overalls, gloves and underwear are tested to protect against fire for several seconds.

    Cornering physics

    Drivers brake from over 300 km/h in a few seconds, then carry huge speed through corners. The racing line uses the full width of the track (outside → apex → outside) to make the corner as gentle as possible.

    Speed0km/h
    Phase—
    G-force0.0g
    • Braking zone: peak deceleration exceeds 5 g. Carbon brakes glow at around 1,000 °C.
    • Apex: the innermost point of the line and usually the slowest point.
    • Exit: early, smooth throttle matters most because it carries speed down the next straight.

    Slipstream vs dirty air

    Following closely has two opposite effects. The car behind has less drag (the slipstream, or tow) on straights, but less downforce in corners because the leader's turbulent wake ("dirty air") disrupts its wings.

    11

    Seven decades of change

    The rules change constantly, for speed, safety, cost and better racing. Scroll through the eras and watch the car transform.

    1950s

    Front-engined pioneers

    The championship began in 1950. Engines sat in front of the driver, tyres were tall and narrow with tread, and there were no wings and no seat belts. Drivers wore cloth or leather helmets early in the decade. Late in the 1950s, rear-engined cars (Cooper) changed everything.

    No aero wingsWire wheelsTreaded tyresConstructors' title from 1958

    Late 1970s

    Wings and ground effect

    Wings arrived in 1968. In 1977–78 designers shaped the sidepods like upside-down wings and sealed them to the track with sliding skirts. The first ground-effect era was born. Downforce and cornering speeds soared, and skirts were banned; flat floors were required from 1983. The first turbo engines also arrived in 1977.

    Sliding skirtsExposed engineHuge rear slicksTurbo era 1977–88

    2000s

    The V10 scream

    3.0-litre V10 engines revved beyond 19,000 rpm. From 1998 cars were narrower (1.8 m) and ran grooved tyres to cut grip. High noses fed air under the car. V8s replaced V10s in 2006, and slicks returned in 2009.

    V10 mandatory 2000–05Grooved tyres 1998–2008Raised noseRefuelling (1994–2009)

    2022–2025

    Ground effect returns

    Hybrid V6 turbo power units arrived in 2014, the halo in 2018. In 2022 the aero was rewritten around underfloor tunnels to reduce dirty air and help cars follow each other. 18-inch wheels arrived too. 2026 brought smaller, lighter cars with active aero and ~50% electric power.
